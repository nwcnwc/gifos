How this meeting works

This meeting has no size limit. Two people or two million, it runs the same. The dream it is built for: the whole Earth in one meeting at once — everyone able to speak, everyone sharing one live room. It scales by folding the crowd into itself, so your phone only ever talks to a handful of neighbors no matter how vast the room grows.

The stadium — how a giant room is arranged

Picture a stadium. You don't meet a million faces; you meet the few around you, and the rest is a living backdrop.

  • Seat — one person.
  • Stage — the front. Whoever steps up here is seen and heard first by everyone in the whole stadium (the reader, a speaker, a shared app).
  • Row — the handful of people right next to you. This is your real conversation — you're directly connected to them.
  • The stadium — everyone beyond your row, packed into one mosaic of equal-size squares that grows downward as the room fills, plus a single summed roar. A million-voice “Amen” reaches you as one sound.

How does it scale? Each row automatically combines its members' video and voices, and rows fold up the tree into one Stadium mosaic. You always see how many people are here — but no single phone ever has to carry more than its own little corner of the room, which is why two people and two million feel exactly the same.

The screen — one endless scroll

Top to bottom, nearest first: the stage, then your row, and finally the stadium — everyone else as equal squares, each person the same size, the grid growing taller the more people arrive. Past about a hundred people the squares shrink into a live tapestry (a small green dot marks who is speaking). Tap any folded tile to zoom it larger; tap again to fold it back.

The sound mix — faders that grow in

Tap Settings for your own private sound board (it only changes what you hear — like your own volume knob). Faders appear as the room gets big enough to need them:

  • Stage — the front of the room.
  • My row — the people right beside you.
  • The stadium — the far roar of everyone else (appears once the room spans past your row). Turn it down during a quiet reading, then push it up to let the whole crowd's response wash in.

Below the faders sits Timing. Sound takes a moment to cross the internet, and this chooses what to do with that moment:

  • Conversation (the usual) — replies come back as fast as your own connection allows. Best for talking.
  • Unison — every phone waits just long enough that the whole room hears everything at the same instant. Shared laughter, an “Amen,” a reading answered together — they actually land together. The trade: replies arrive a blink later, set by the slowest connection in the room.
  • 🎵 Song — tap it to lead the room in song yourself; it stays lit for everyone while a song is on (see below).

Singing together — 🎵

Singing on a normal video call is famously awful: every voice arrives a different beat late, so a hymn turns to mush — and phone microphones treat a held note like background noise and try to erase it. This room does it differently.

When someone taps 🎵 Song (in Settings, under Timing), they step onto the stage as the song leader, their microphone switches to music quality, and every phone quietly lines the room up in time — the way sound behaves in a great stone church:

  • Everyone hears the leader at the same moment, so the whole room is truly singing together, wherever everyone is.
  • The voices near you arrive as one choir, a beat behind your own — like the room answering around you.
  • The leader hears the congregation answer from behind, the way a cantor hears a cathedral.

Earbuds matter. Any earbuds — even the cheap ones in a drawer — let your phone capture just your voice, making you part of the choir at full quality. No earbuds? Sing along anyway on speaker: you'll hear everything, and your voice joins as part of the crowd. The song ends when the leader taps 🎵 again or steps off the stage, and everything returns to normal by itself.

The buttons worth knowing

  • Blur (Max / Min / None) — how blurred your own video looks to everyone. You always control your own camera; nobody can un-blur you.
  • Stage — step up to be seen and heard first by the whole room; tap again to step down.
  • Hand — raise it and you float to the top of a room-wide queue, in order.
  • Password — the key to clear video. Set one and faces sharpen for those who have it; without one, everyone stays gently blurred.
  • Invite — one link brings people straight in.
  • Run app — share an app (a Bible, a board, a game) that everyone in the room uses together, live.
  • Record / CC — record to your own device, or turn on live captions your phone writes from your own voice.
  • Admin — some rooms have a host who can moderate; most rooms are open and run on friendly anarchy.

Stepping away

Flip to another tab or app and you stay in the meeting: you keep hearing everyone, everyone keeps hearing you, and your tile politely shows “away” so nobody wonders. Your camera pauses while you're gone — phones switch cameras off for hidden apps to save battery — and comes back the moment you return. On phones, the ongoing-call controls (mute, hang up) appear with your device's own media controls.

Your privacy

Your picture and your voice go straight to the other people in the room — there is no company in the middle. They never pass through a computer of ours: we run none for video, and the one small helper that introduces phones to each other is deliberately too slow to carry a picture or a sound.

Everything is scrambled on the way, with the same kind of lock a bank uses, so the only people who can see or hear you are the people in the room with you. That little helper is told as little as possible: never your video, your voice, your name, the room's password, or even which room you are in. (In a very big room, to save everyone's data, some of the far-away faces are bundled together and passed along by other people in the same room — still never a company's computer, and never anyone outside the room.)

The one honest trade for having no middleman: because phones connect straight to each other, everyone on a call can see everyone's internet address — nobody here is anonymous. Tap the status text to see the list.
